Credit Suisse: how $17 billion of bonds were wiped out in one weekend

29 October 2026 · watch time 12 min · Watch on YouTube

On Sunday, March 19, 2023, at 10:01 p.m., one order went out. About sixteen billion Swiss francs of Credit Suisse bonds, roughly $17 billion, were written down to zero. The shareholders, who normally lose first, still got three billion francs. Two and a half years later a Swiss court said the write-down lacked legal basis. This is how AT1 bonds work, and how two words in the small print made it possible.

What an AT1 bond is

After the financial crisis, regulators wrote a rule for banks: if a bank needs rescuing, some of its bonds must take the loss, written off or turned into shares. Every bank stacks its money in a fixed order, and shareholders take losses first. Just above them sits Additional Tier 1, or AT1: a bond with a high coupon and no maturity date.

Credit Suisse's AT1s had two triggers. The first was automatic: core capital falling below a set level, 7% on most of them. The second was a judgment call: the regulator declaring a Viability Event, including the case where the state promises extraordinary support that shores up the bank's capital. By 2023 the market for these bonds was worth about $275 billion.

In June 2022 Credit Suisse sold a new AT1 of its own, running its own book as sole lead, with a 9.75% coupon. It raised $1.65 billion. Nine months later that bond was worth nothing. It was one of more than a dozen Credit Suisse AT1s, together about sixteen billion Swiss francs.

The run

The trouble started long before that weekend. In March 2021 Credit Suisse froze $10 billion of funds linked to Greensill; weeks later Archegos, a family-office client, blew up and the bank lost $5.5 billion. In 2022 it lost 7.3 billion francs, and clients pulled 110 billion francs in the last three months of the year alone. Yet on paper its capital was fine: 14.1%, twice the trigger. The problem was money walking out of the door.

In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank collapsed. Credit Suisse published its annual report admitting material weaknesses in its financial reporting controls; its top shareholder, Saudi National Bank, ruled out putting in more money. The shares fell 24% in a day. The bank borrowed up to 50 billion francs from the central bank, and by Friday the shares closed at 1.86 francs. Swiss officials had one weekend to broker a sale.

The weekend

On Sunday UBS made a first offer of up to one billion dollars. At 4:24 p.m. Credit Suisse wrote to FINMA, the Swiss regulator, asking it not to declare a Viability Event: a write-down would be a gift of about 16 billion francs to the buyer's shareholders. At 6:24 FINMA replied that if Credit Suisse would not write the bonds down, FINMA would order it. At 8 p.m. the government amended its emergency ordinance: new article 5a let FINMA order AT1s written down. The explicit power was written two hours before it was used.

That evening UBS agreed to buy Credit Suisse for three billion francs in its own shares, 76 centimes per share, with no shareholder vote. The state offered up to nine billion francs to cover losses after UBS took the first five, and up to 200 billion francs of central-bank liquidity; at the peak the central bank's loans reached 168 billion francs. At 10:01 p.m. FINMA's order reached Credit Suisse: every AT1 written down to zero, citing the extraordinary state support. On Monday the bonds were bid as low as one cent; European regulators said that in the EU shares are the first to absorb losses. It was the largest AT1 loss ever.

The verdict

UBS reported a $29 billion profit in its first quarter with Credit Suisse, essentially all negative goodwill: it paid far less than the fair value of the net assets. The state earned about 200 million francs in fees. Nearly eight months after the wipeout, UBS sold $3.5 billion of its own AT1s at 9.25%, with orders above $36 billion.

About 3,000 investors took FINMA to court. In October 2025 Switzerland's Federal Administrative Court ruled in the first case that the write-down lacked legal basis: the contract's Viability Event had not been triggered and article 5a was unconstitutional. FINMA and UBS appealed to the Federal Supreme Court, which in December 2025 let the write-down stand until it rules. A suit in New York failed on sovereign immunity, and Japanese investors took Switzerland to international arbitration.

The Ledger

  1. An AT1 pays like a bond and can die like a share. The coupon is the price of that switch.
  2. Read who holds the switch: here, a regulator, on a Sunday, with a power written two hours earlier.
  3. The order of losses is a promise. Shareholders got three billion francs, the bonds above them got zero, and a court is still deciding.
