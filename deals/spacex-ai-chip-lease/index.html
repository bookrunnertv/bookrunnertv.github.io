Why SpaceX is renting its AI chips instead of buying them

11 November 2026 · watch time 6 min · Watch on YouTube

SpaceX has lined up about $40 billion for Nvidia's next AI chips — but it won't buy them. An entity managed by Apollo will, and rent them to SpaceX under a finance lease. Here's who puts in the money, why the rent is "absolute", and who really carries the risk.

SpaceX won't buy its chips

SpaceX has lined up about 40 billion dollars for Nvidia's next AI chips. But SpaceX won't buy them. A company managed by Apollo will, and rent them to SpaceX. And SpaceX must pay that rent no matter what, says a presentation seen by Bloomberg. Even if the chips break, or a disaster hits the data center, it says. Now, Bloomberg reports, Wall Street is offering slices of the deal to investors. So if those chips are worth far less by 2032 who's left holding the bill?

“Wait. Why would SpaceX rent chips instead of buying them?”

By the end, you'll know who carries the risk, and why.

Why rent the chips?

SpaceX's AI business is growing fast. In the third quarter, it brought in 7.6 billion dollars. That's more than half of SpaceX's 14.6 billion dollars of sales. But AI runs on chips, and chips are expensive. In that quarter alone, SpaceX spent 19.6 billion dollars on equipment and buildings. That's almost three times as much as a year earlier. And its finance chief expects to spend 35 to 40 billion dollars in this quarter alone.

“That's a lot of cash. Where does it come from?”

One answer: don't buy the chips. Rent them. SpaceX committed to a finance lease: rent that, over time, pays off the chips' full cost. Every quarter, SpaceX pays one twentieth of the cost, plus interest at a fixed rate. Twenty quarters make five years. By then, the cost is paid back.

“So it's really a loan, paid as rent?”

In practice, it works like one. And that's where Wall Street comes in.

Who puts in the money

First, the owner of the chips: a company managed by Apollo. Bloomberg calls it a special purpose vehicle. Bloomberg reported it will buy about 47 billion dollars of Nvidia Rubin chips and related gear. Per the same report, SpaceX pays about 7 billion dollars of the rent up front. The rest, about 40 billion dollars, the vehicle borrows, the same report says. Bloomberg says 8.3 billion is a loan led by Goldman Sachs, for syndication to other banks.

The rest comes from bonds for big investment funds, the same report says. Apollo is a key buyer, and Pimco took part too, Bloomberg reported. Funds run by BlackRock, Goldman's fund arm and accounts advised by KKR signed up first, as anchors. Put together: chips go to SpaceX, rent goes to the vehicle, and the vehicle pays its lenders.

“Why put the debt in a separate company at all?”

The structure is meant to keep the debt off SpaceX's balance sheet, Bloomberg reported. SpaceX's own filing calls it a finance lease. By early November, Goldman and Apollo had begun offering slices of up to 500 million dollars, Bloomberg reported.

The rent is "absolute"

Here's the twist. A presentation seen by Bloomberg says: SpaceX's obligation to pay rent is absolute. The same presentation says rent is owed whatever the chips' condition, even after an accident or force majeure.

“Even if a fire wrecks the data center?”

Per that presentation, the rent is owed even then. And SpaceX gives no guarantee on the chips' residual value, Bloomberg reported. The deal hasn't closed yet, and the interest rate isn't set. The rate gets set at pricing, before signing. Semafor reported the priciest slice could cost up to 8.75 percent a year. Lenders agreed before the deal was priced, which leaves them exposed to the market, Bloomberg reported. Banks know that risk: in 2022, seven banks got stuck with Twitter's 13 billion dollars of debt.

Who carries the risk

Now jump to around 2032. These Rubin chips will be about five years old. If SpaceX keeps paying, the rent pays back the full cost, and the lenders get paid. As long as SpaceX pays, the chips' age is SpaceX's problem.

“And if SpaceX stops paying?”

Then the lenders would lean largely on the chips, and chips tend to lose value as newer ones arrive. So the lenders' main bet isn't the chips. It's SpaceX, as the presentation Bloomberg saw puts it. The cost of insuring SpaceX's debt rose from 150 to 282 basis points in two months, ICE data shows.

“So insuring against a SpaceX default costs almost twice as much?”

Almost twice, yes. The price of that bet went up.
