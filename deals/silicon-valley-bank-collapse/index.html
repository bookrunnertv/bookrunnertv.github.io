Silicon Valley Bank: the $2.25 billion share sale that started a run

12 November 2026 · watch time 9 min · Watch on YouTube

On Thursday, March 9, 2023, 42 billion dollars left a single bank in one day: about a million dollars a second, by its CEO's count. The night before, Silicon Valley Bank had announced a $2.25 billion share sale, led by Goldman Sachs, the same bank that had just bought SVB's $21 billion bond portfolio, at an after-tax loss to SVB of $1.8 billion. Depositors read it as a sign of trouble, and ran. On Friday morning regulators shut the bank down, and the share sale died with it. This is how a bank that looked fine on paper died in about 42 hours, why a fix can start a run, and who came out ahead.

The hole

Start in 2021. Start-ups were flush with venture money, and SVB's deposits roughly tripled in two years, to $189 billion. It put much of that cash into long-term bonds: $98 billion by the end of 2021. Bonds are safe from default, but not from rising interest rates. And rates did rise: eight Fed hikes in under a year, from near zero to a range of 4.5 to 4.75%.

Picture a bond paying a low, fixed interest. Then rates rise, and new bonds pay more. To sell yours now, you'd have to cut its price. That drop is an unrealized loss: real only if you sell.

Paper loss on its long-term bonds≈15
The owners' entire stake (equity)16

Almost the same size. Was SVB almost broke? Only if it had to sell, and the rules kept that loss out of its capital ratios. So SVB looked fine, as long as nobody forced it to sell. Who could? Its depositors.

The FDIC insures each depositor up to $250,000. At SVB, about nine in every ten dollars sat above that limit: uninsured. Insured depositors can wait. Uninsured ones have every reason to leave first. If enough leave at once, the bank must sell the bonds, and the paper loss turns real.

The fix

Early 2023. Cash kept draining, and Moody's warned SVB of a possible downgrade. SVB brought in Goldman Sachs to help it raise capital. Step one, on March 8: sell the bonds it kept ‘available for sale’. The buyer: Goldman Sachs. It paid $21.45 billion for bonds SVB carried at about $24 billion. SVB's loss, after tax: about $1.8 billion.

Its CEO, Greg Becker, later told the Senate they sold first ‘based on the advice of Goldman Sachs’. Goldman says it told SVB in writing that it was not SVB's adviser on that sale, that the bond price came from arm's-length talks, and that SVB's board approved it.

Step two, that evening: launch a $2.25 billion sale of new shares. Most from the public, and $500 million from one investor, General Atlantic. Goldman ran the book: it collected investors' orders and proposed the price. Beside it sat SVB Securities, SVB's own investment-banking arm. So Goldman bought the bonds and ran the share sale. On Wednesday, March 8, after the close, the plan went public in a filing stamped 5:16 p.m. New York time. The clock started.

The run

Now you're a start-up with your cash at SVB. Your bank just took a loss and is asking for capital. Stay, or go? The Fed's then top supervisor said uninsured depositors took the moves as ‘a signal that the bank was in distress’. The capital raise told everyone there was a hole. If you were uninsured, leaving cost little, and staying could cost a lot.

On Thursday, March 9, some venture firms told their start-ups to pull their cash. Founders Fund, Bloomberg reported, saw no downside to leaving. SVB's shares fell 60%, their worst day on record. By the end of Thursday, $42 billion had left: roughly a quarter of its deposits. Forty-two billion in a day? Big clients. SVB's ten largest accounts alone held $13.3 billion.

That evening, the Fed told the FDIC that SVB was unlikely to have enough cash. For Friday, about $100 billion more was queued to leave. Withdrawn or queued, in under two days: about $142 billion, more than 80% of its deposits. On Friday at 11:15 a.m. New York time, California's regulator closed the bank and handed it to the FDIC, about 42 hours after the plan went public. The share sale was called off, never priced. General Atlantic's $500 million never arrived.

Who came out ahead

On Sunday, March 12, the Treasury, the Fed and the FDIC stepped in. Every depositor, insured or not, could have all their money on Monday. SVB's shareholders got no such protection. The bill: about $20 billion, by the FDIC's first estimate. Banks, not taxpayers, refill the insurance fund, partly through a special fee.

Two weeks later, the FDIC sold SVB's deposits and loans to First Citizens. It got a $16.5 billion discount on about $72 billion of assets. Its gain on the deal, after tax: $9.8 billion, in a single quarter.

And Goldman? Senator Elizabeth Warren accused it of ‘raking in fees’ on the failed sale. Goldman's reply: by its own account, no underwriting fee at all, and an expected gain of about $60 million on the bonds.

And SVB itself? The Fed's then top supervisor called it ‘a textbook case of mismanagement’. His review also faulted Fed supervisors for not making SVB fix its problems fast enough.

The Ledger

  1. On paper, SVB was fine, but only while its depositors stayed.
  2. A capital raise is also a message. Here, depositors heard ‘hole’, not ‘fix’.
  3. By Goldman's account, the failed sale earned it no fee. First Citizens, buying SVB's loans and deposits, booked a gain of almost $10 billion.
