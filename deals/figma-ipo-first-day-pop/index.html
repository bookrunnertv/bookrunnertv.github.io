Figma IPO: $3.5 billion left on the table, by Bloomberg's count

5 November 2026 · watch time 12 min · Watch on YouTube

On the evening of July 30, 2025, the banks priced Figma's shares at $33. The next day, its first on the stock market, the stock closed at $115.50: up 250%, the biggest first-day jump in at least 30 years for a US IPO raising over a billion dollars, by Bloomberg's count. On paper, more than three and a half billion dollars was left on the table. By April 2026 the shares had sunk to $16.60. This is the anatomy of Figma's IPO: was $33 too cheap, or $115.50 too expensive? And who paid for it?

The basics: the book and the pop

An IPO is a company's first sale of shares to the public. Think of a concert presale: fans say how many tickets they want, and the most they would pay. In an IPO the banks do that with big investors. Then they set one price for all, and pick who gets shares. That is bookbuilding. More orders than shares, and the book is oversubscribed.

Why not just sell to whoever pays the most? Because the seller also picks its owners. Figma reportedly wanted long-term backers.

Now say you sell your car for ten thousand dollars, and that evening the buyer resells it for thirty-five thousand. That extra twenty-five thousand went to the buyer, not to you. In an IPO that jump is the pop. Times every share sold, it is the money left on the table. Unless that evening price was too high.

The whole deal in one picture: Figma sells new shares and keeps that cash. Its existing owners sell some of theirs, and that cash goes to them. In the middle, the bookrunner runs the book and places every share with the funds, at one price.

The $20 billion breakup

It starts three years earlier, with a buyer. In September 2022 Adobe, run by Shantanu Narayen, agreed to buy Figma for about $20 billion. Then competition regulators stepped in, in Brussels, London and Washington. In December 2023, fifteen months in, both sides walked away. Their reason: no clear path to approval.

Adobe paid Figma a one-billion-dollar termination fee: the price of walking away. Figma kept the billion, but lost its price tag. Without a buyer, what was it worth?

In April 2025 Figma filed in private to go public instead. Its banks were hired, with Morgan Stanley leading them as bookrunner. On July 1 the filing went public. One detail: Dylan Field's shares carry 15 votes each, and with a co-founder's votes he controls about three quarters of the votes. Adobe's deal had died; now the banks' book would set Figma's price.

Building the book

On July 21 the roadshow began: the banks pitched Figma to big funds at $25 to $28 a share. Each fund was asked how many shares it wanted and the most it would pay; for Figma, the banks reportedly insisted on both. The orders poured in, and on July 28 the banks raised the range to $30 to $32. Demand was close to forty times the shares on offer, Bloomberg reported.

Then why not charge a lot more? Because price is only half the decision. The other half is who gets the shares. Figma wanted long-term owners, and courting them kept the price in check, Bloomberg reported. On the evening of July 30 the price was set at $33, a dollar above the raised range: about 37 million shares, for $1.2 billion.

Who got them? Reportedly, about half the investors who ordered got nothing, and about 25 investors took 70% of the shares, IFR reported. On July 31, on the New York Stock Exchange, the first trade was at $85. The close: $115.50, up 250% in a day. Demand was so strong that the banks sold 5.5 million extra shares from the sellers, as the deal allowed, at the same $33. In all, about 42.5 million shares were sold.

Who left the money on the table?

Start with one share. Sold at $33, it closed the day at $115.50: a gap of $82.50. Like the car. Now multiply by every share sold in the IPO, about 42.5 million: about $3.5 billion. Bloomberg's own count: more than $3.5 billion.

By our count, then, the sellers left the most: about 70% of it. The biggest single seller was a charity, the Marin Community Foundation. It sold about 13.4 million shares, for about $440 million. At the first-day close, those same shares were worth about one and a half billion.

The banks' fee: about $63 million. The money left on the table was about 56 times as much. Critics say that's the problem. Venture capitalist Bill Gurley, who has called the IPO process archaic, said the pop was ‘expected and fully intentional’; their argument is that a low price pleases the big funds the banks deal with every day. The other side, per Bloomberg: Figma's pursuit of long-term backers kept the price in check. And Field, on day one: a share price is ‘a moment in time’.

How we count: money left on the table is an on-paper measure, first-day close minus IPO price, times shares sold. We include the 5.5 million extra (greenshoe) shares, the same basis as Bloomberg's figure; on the base deal alone it is about $3.05 billion, the figure in Jay Ritter's IPO data. The split between Figma and the selling holders is our own calculation.

After the bell

Then the stock slid. By the end of August 2025 the shares closed the month near $70. On November 21, 2025, Figma dipped below its $33 IPO price for the first time, to $32.83. At that low, anyone who bought at the first-day close was down over 70%.

In April 2026, reported fears of AI competition hit the stock. At its low it touched $16.60: even the funds that got shares at $33 were down about half. Yet the business itself kept growing fast. In the three months to June 2026, revenue was up 48% on a year earlier.

So was the IPO too cheap, or was day one too expensive? Both, depending on when you look. Against the first-day close, $33 was too low. Against April 2026, $115.50 was far too high.

The Ledger

  2. About $3.5 billion was left on the table, mostly by the sellers: 56 times the banks' fee.
  3. Day one is a moment in time. By April 2026, the shares traded at half the IPO price.
