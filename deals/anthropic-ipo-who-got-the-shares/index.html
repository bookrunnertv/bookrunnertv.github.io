Anthropic's $81 billion IPO: who got the money, the shares and the fees

Anthropic priced its IPO on November 18 at $83 a share: 980 million shares, about $81 billion by our sum. Who got the money, who got the shares, and who got paid to sell them? Explained from zero, from Anthropic's filings and its pricing announcement.

Anthropic's $81 billion IPO

Last night, Anthropic priced its IPO: about 81 billion dollars' worth of shares. CNBC calls it the largest stock offering on record, ahead of SpaceX.

“So could anyone buy a share at that price?”

Not quite. It works like tickets to a sold-out concert. The price is set the night before, and the banks running the deal decide who gets one. So, three questions. One: who got the money? Two: who got the shares? And three: who got paid to sell them? That one is at the end.

Who got the money?

The price: 83 dollars a share. The filing had said 70 to 78. And the deal grew. The plan was 825 million shares. The final offering: 980 million. At 83 dollars, that's about 81 billion dollars, before the banks' fees. That's our own sum.

“Does all of that go to Anthropic?”

Almost all. Anthropic itself sold about 956 million shares: about 79 billion dollars, by our sum. The rest came from its seven co-founders. They each sold about 3.4 million shares, about 280 million dollars each, by our count. That money goes to them, the co-founders. Not to the company. Then there's a second sale, outside the IPO. Seven tech companies have agreed to buy 26 billion dollars of shares, at the same 83 dollars.

Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC. Their shares come with no vote. So the company is set to take in about 105 billion dollars before fees. 79 from the IPO, 26 from those seven. Again, our sum. And the banks have 30 days to buy 147 million more shares from Anthropic. Hold that thought.

Who got the shares?

More buyers wanted in than there were shares. A week before, Bloomberg reported orders for about six times the offering.

“So who decides who gets them?”

The banks running the deal. And the price comes from a negotiation, in the filing's words “among us, the selling stockholders, and the representatives.” Those representatives are Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, at the head of 22 banks. Every order goes into one list, called the book: who wants shares, how many, at what price. Then the banks hand out the shares from it. Retail investors, people investing on their own, could get up to 20 percent of the shares.

Through brokers like Schwab, Fidelity and Robinhood. With no promise: the IPO website said nobody was sure to get any. Then there are the big funds. Bloomberg reported that existing investors such as Sequoia and Lightspeed planned to buy billions. And some of Anthropic's board members and their firms said they might buy up to 303 million dollars of shares. Not a binding promise.

“And if I didn't get any?”

So the 83-dollar shares went to that list. Everyone else can buy from today, on the market, at whatever price it trades. One at a time, in plain English.

Who got paid to sell them?

The banks' fee is called the underwriting discount. It's a slice of the IPO money. And all of it goes to the 22 banks. The final figure goes in the final prospectus. On 27 October, before there was a price range, an Evercore ISI analyst estimated about 560 million dollars for all the banks. His base case: about 112 million dollars each for Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley. And the 26 billion from the seven tech companies? No fee for the banks. The filing says so.

“So the banks just take a fee and leave?”

Not right away. Remember those 147 million extra shares? That option is called the greenshoe. When the shares are handed out, the banks may sell more than 980 million, before they own the extra ones. To cover the extra shares, the banks buy in the market or use the option. Buying in the market, the filing says, may keep the price higher than it would otherwise be.

“So which one do they pick?”

They weigh the market price against the option's 83 dollars. And the filing says none of this is required: they can stop at any time. So the banks get paid to sell. And for a while, they may also trade to stabilize the price.
