How Elon Musk's $44 billion Twitter deal was actually financed

26 October 2026 · watch time 12 min · Watch on YouTube

In October 2022, seven of the world's biggest banks wired $13 billion to fund one man's takeover of Twitter. Within weeks, buyers were offering 60 cents on the dollar for that debt. The banks refused to sell, and kept it for thirty months. This is how the $44 billion deal was put together, and why three words in a commitment letter, committed to provide, trapped the lenders.

The target

In 2021 Twitter made $5.08 billion in revenue. It had 217 million daily users and more than 7,500 employees, and it still lost money: $221 million that year. That company was about to borrow $13 billion.

On April 4, 2022, Elon Musk disclosed a 9.2% stake. Ten days later he offered $54.20 a share, a 38% premium to the April 1 price. On April 20, seven banks signed a commitment letter, and on April 25 Twitter's board accepted. Total value: about $44 billion, agreed in three weeks. For a deal of that size, that is almost unheard of.

Sources and uses

Musk did not buy Twitter himself: a vehicle called X Holdings did. The definitive merger proxy put the total funds needed at about $46.5 billion: $44 billion to buy the shares, and the rest for fees, expenses and other items.

Most of the money, then, was Musk's. The banks matter because of one phrase in their commitment letter: they were committed to provide the $13 billion. They did not promise to try to find it; they promised to fund it. That is underwriting: if the market closes tomorrow, you still wire the money.

The $13 billion, in four slices

The order matters: the secured pieces get paid before the unsecured one if things go wrong.

A bridge is money banks lend for a short period, expecting to replace it quickly with bonds sold to investors. If the bonds never sell, the bridge stays, and its interest rate steps up quarter after quarter.

The commitment letter names the seven lenders and each bank's share. Morgan Stanley led with about 27%, roughly $3.5 billion. Bank of America, Barclays and MUFG took about 21% each. BNP Paribas, Mizuho and Société Générale took the rest. The plan, as always, was syndication: sell most of the loans to investors within weeks of closing and keep the fees.

The margin loan that vanished

The original plan also included a $12.5 billion margin loan to a Musk holding company, secured on his Tesla shares. Within five weeks it was gone: co-investors came in with about $7 billion, Musk raised his equity commitment to $33.5 billion, and the margin loan was terminated. The $13 billion of bank debt never moved.

The co-investors included Larry Ellison ($1 billion), Sequoia ($800 million), Binance ($500 million) and Qatar's sovereign fund ($375 million). Prince Alwaleed's Kingdom Holding rolled its existing Twitter shares into the new company, worth about $1.9 billion at the deal price. Equity investors can lose everything, but they are never forced to sell. A bank that means to sell a loan has to mark it to market, quarter after quarter.

The twist: the buyer tries to walk away

On July 8, 2022, Musk's lawyers wrote to Twitter that he was terminating the deal, citing its data on spam accounts. Twitter sued in the Delaware Court of Chancery and asked the court to force the purchase. The trial was set for October 17.

Meanwhile central banks raised interest rates sharply. Analysts estimated the new debt would cost Twitter close to $1.2 billion a year in interest, against less than $100 million before the deal. Investors in leveraged loans concluded the debt was too cheap for the risk.

On October 3, two weeks before trial, Musk's side wrote that he would close at the original price, pending receipt of the proceeds of the debt financing. The judge paused the trial and the merger became effective on October 27, 2022. Days earlier, the Wall Street Journal and Reuters reported that the banks would not try to sell the debt: they would keep all $13 billion on their own balance sheets. Bankers call that a hung deal.

How bad it got

In October 2022 Bloomberg estimated a loss of at least $500 million if the banks sold at market. By December, Reuters reported the secured debt would fetch no more than 60 cents on the dollar, and the unsecured piece even less. In January 2023 one Bloomberg calculation put the group's paper losses near $4 billion. The banks never confirmed a figure. For scale, that autumn a bank group lost about $700 million selling the debt behind the Citrix buyout.

Twitter, renamed X, kept paying interest: the first payment, about $300 million, arrived in January 2023. Some banks marked the loans as low as 65 cents, according to the Wall Street Journal, which in August 2024 called it the worst buyout for banks since the financial crisis.

The way out

What changed was not the debt but the borrower. In early 2025 demand for anything linked to Musk surged. In February the banks sold a $5.5 billion term loan at 97 cents and $4.74 billion of fixed-rate secured loans at par, with a 9.5% coupon. In April the last $1.2 billion went at 98 cents. Thirty months after closing, the banks were out.

On the principal they sold at roughly 95 to 100 cents. Bloomberg estimated the group collected around $3 billion of interest while it waited; the banks have not disclosed the figure. The real cost was two and a half years of capital locked up. Twitter's own advisers were paid well (Goldman Sachs stood to earn $80 million, JPMorgan $53 million); the lenders' arrangement fees were never disclosed.

  2. A bridge is only safe if the other bank exists: a bond market willing to take the loans off your hands.
