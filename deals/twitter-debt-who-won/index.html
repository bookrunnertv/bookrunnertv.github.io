Elon Musk bought Twitter with $13 billion of debt: who won?

20 November 2026 · watch time 9 min · Watch on YouTube

The debt behind Elon Musk's Twitter buyout, explained from zero. In 2022, seven banks promised $13 billion of loans so Elon Musk could buy Twitter. They meant to sell those loans on to investors. Instead, they got stuck with them for two and a half years. Where did that $13 billion sit, what did the wait cost, and when every dollar was repaid in 2026, who actually won?

Stuck with $13 billion

Elon Musk buys Twitter with 13 billion dollars of borrowed money. The banks that lent it meant to sell those loans on, and got stuck with them instead. The loans stay with the banks for two and a half years.

“So the banks lost a fortune, right?”

The headlines thought so. The Wall Street Journal called it the worst buyout for banks since the financial crisis. But the loans do sell. And in 2026, every dollar is repaid, years early, with a penalty of 425 million dollars on top. So, three questions. One: where did those 13 billion actually sit? Two: what did the wait cost? And three: when it ended, who actually won? Stay to the end and you will know.

Where did the $13bn sit?

In April 2022, Elon Musk agrees to buy Twitter for 44 billion dollars. Part of that money is borrowed. Seven banks promise to lend 13 billion dollars. And here is the first surprise. Those loans are not Musk's to repay. They land on Twitter itself.

“Wait. The company pays for its own purchase?”

Yes. That is a leveraged buyout. You buy a company with loans, and the company itself has to repay them. Now, the banks. In a deal like this, a bank is a shop, not a collector. It promises the whole loan first. Then it plans to sell it on to investors, and keep a fee. And that promise is binding. If no investor shows up, the banks still have to hand over all 13 billion. Think of those 13 billion as stock you ordered for your shop: bought to be sold, not to be kept.

“And if nobody buys?”

Bankers call that a hung deal. The loans they promised stay on their own shelf, unsold, and they have to keep them. The 13 billion came in pieces. The biggest was the main loan: 6.5 billion dollars. Then two bridge loans of 3 billion dollars each: temporary loans, meant to be replaced by longer-term debt. And a credit line of half a billion. And all of it was split among seven banks. Morgan Stanley took the most: about 3.5 billion dollars.

That is one bank, on one deal. Bank of America, Barclays and MUFG took about 2.7 billion each. BNP Paribas, Mizuho and Société Générale shared the last 1.4 billion. Keep an eye on the two bridges. A bridge loan gets more expensive the longer you keep it, up to a cap. Years later, the document SpaceX filed to go public showed the caps. 9.5 percent on the safer bridge, 13 percent on the riskier one. On October 27, 2022, the deal closes. The banks hand over the money, and the sale of the loans is supposed to begin.

What did the wait cost?

The trouble starts early. Even before the deal closed, Bloomberg had done the sums. Selling the loans at once would cost the banks 500 million dollars or more. By November, some funds do offer to buy Twitter's loans. But Bloomberg reported the price: as little as 60 cents on the dollar. Cents on the dollar is how a loan is priced. Say you lent 100 dollars, and a buyer offers you 60 for that loan. Sell, and you lose 40 on the spot.

On 13 billion dollars, a price like that means billions. Bloomberg estimated the loss on paper at around 4 billion dollars. And Twitter is not alone. By January 2023, Bloomberg counts about 40 billion dollars of buyout debt that banks cannot sell. Twitter by itself is about a third of it. Put yourself in the banks' seat: about 13 billion out, and offers as low as 60 cents.

“So why not just sell and move on?”

Because a loss on paper only becomes final when you sell. And the banks, Bloomberg reported, refused to go below 70 cents. So the loans go on the shelf. And here is the strange part: while it sat there, this stock paid them. Before the buyout, Twitter paid about 50 million dollars a year in interest. After it: more than a billion, Bloomberg reported. So the banks wait. All through 2023. More than a year in, several investors tell the Financial Times they would not buy at any price.

By August 2024, the loans have been stuck for almost two years. The Wall Street Journal reports a record: longer than any buyout loans since the financial crisis. And waiting has a cost of its own. Money tied up in one loan is money you cannot lend to anyone else.

How did the banks get out?

“What changed? It's the same company.”

Its name, as you know: Twitter is now called X. And Bloomberg reported what the banks showed investors: X's latest earnings, and its stake in xAI, Musk's AI company. In January, the banks test the water. They sell about 1 billion dollars of the main loan in private, at 90 to 95 cents. In February they offer about 3 billion dollars more. So many investors show up that they sell 5.5 billion, at 97 cents. 97 cents, for loans that once drew offers of 60.

Now the hard part: the two bridges. The banks fold most of them into one new loan backed by X's assets, with a fixed interest rate of 9.5 percent. Why does fixed matter? The main loan's rate moves with the market. A fixed rate never moves: you know exactly what you will be paid. A week later they sell 4.74 billion dollars of it at full price: 100 cents on the dollar.

“Full price? For the bridges?”

Yes. Investors asked for more than the banks first offered: the sale grew from about 3 billion dollars to 4.74. In April 2025 the last piece goes: about 1.2 billion dollars, at 98 cents. 30 months after the deal closed, the shelf is empty. If you add up the discounts on every sale, you get our estimate: between 240 and 290 million dollars. About 2 cents on the dollar. Remember the 4 billion dollar loss on paper? By our estimate, the banks gave up less than a tenth of that.

And while they waited, they collected interest: around 3 billion dollars by early 2025, Bloomberg estimated. That is before the banks' own costs, which they have not published. One more change that spring. In March 2025, xAI buys X, in a deal paid in shares. The deal values X at 45 billion dollars, debt included: about the price of the 2022 buyout. And the debt comes along.

Who actually won?

By now the old Twitter loans belong to investors, no longer to the seven banks. And in February 2026 the owner changes again: SpaceX buys xAI. One month later, SpaceX pays the loans back. It takes a new loan of 20 billion dollars and uses it to clear the main debts of X and xAI. The total: 18.9 billion dollars. About 12.5 billion of it is the old Twitter debt. The rest is xAI's own debt, interest, and penalties. xAI had borrowed about 5 billion dollars of its own in 2025.

“Penalties? For paying a loan back?”

A prepayment penalty works like this: repay a loan early, and you owe the lender extra. It makes up for the interest the lender will no longer earn. For the fixed-rate loan, the contract set the price of leaving early: about 107 cents for every dollar owed. On the old Twitter loans, that penalty came to 425 million dollars. Across X and xAI together, SpaceX paid about 1.2 billion. In its accounts, SpaceX recorded a loss of about 1.5 billion dollars on that early repayment.

“Why pay extra just to repay early?”

One fact: the new money was far cheaper. The fixed-rate loan cost 9.5 percent a year. At the end of March, SpaceX's new loan cost 4.58 percent. So, who actually won? Start with the banks. Stuck for 30 months, they got out at 90 to 100 cents on the dollar. Our view: not a win, an escape. Then the investors who bought in 2025. Say you paid 100 dollars for a piece of the fixed-rate loan that February. About a year later, you had collected about 10 dollars in interest, and you were repaid about 107.

And the bill? It went to the borrower. More than a billion dollars a year in interest, as Bloomberg reported, and then the penalties to leave. So who won? Our read: the 2025 buyers did best, and the banks escaped. The lenders got their money back, or nearly all of it, with interest. The side that paid was the side that borrowed. The banks' real weapon was time. If you must sell at 60 cents, you take the loss. They could wait, and this time it worked.
