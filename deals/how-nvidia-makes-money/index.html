How Nvidia actually makes its money, explained from zero

16 November 2026 · watch time 9 min · Watch on YouTube

You type a question into an AI chatbot, and somewhere a building full of computers goes to work. In three months, Nvidia sold $96 billion, most of it equipment for buildings like that. So what does Nvidia actually sell, who pays for it, and how does it end up with $62 of profit for every $100 it sells? We follow $100 through Nvidia's own filings, from zero.

Where does Nvidia's money come from?

You type a question into an AI chatbot. Somewhere, a building full of computers goes to work on your answer. In just three months, Nvidia's sales reached 96 billion dollars, most of it equipment for buildings like that. That is more than a billion dollars a day.

“Wait. Isn't Nvidia the video game graphics company?”

It was. In its last full year, gaming was about 7 cents of every dollar of sales. So let's follow 100 dollars as they arrive at Nvidia. One: what did the customer actually buy? Two: who is the customer? And three: where do those 100 dollars go? Because Nvidia ends up with a profit of 62.

What does Nvidia sell?

Nvidia was founded in 1993. Its original focus was graphics chips for PCs. In fiscal 2024, a whole year of sales was 61 billion dollars. Now a single quarter is 96 billion, more than that whole year.

“So a GPU is just a chip for games?”

A GPU, a graphics processing unit, is a chip that does a huge number of small calculations at the same time. Games need that. It turned out that AI needs it too. Today Nvidia describes itself differently: as a data center scale AI infrastructure company.

“In plain English, please.”

In plain English: it sells the computing equipment AI runs on. The chips, the links between them, and the software. Take our 100 dollars from the quarter that ended in July 2026. About $92.50 came from data center equipment. That data center figure, 89 billion dollars, was more than double the same quarter a year earlier. The other $7.50 came from everything else: PCs, game consoles, workstations, robotics and cars.

“So they sell a lot of chips.”

Not quite. Nvidia says its data center products are typically delivered as rack-scale systems, subsystems or modules. A rack is a tall cabinet, about the size of a wardrobe, packed with chips and the wiring that links them. And the racks are built to work together. Nvidia says hundreds of thousands of GPUs can be linked to work as one giant computer. So the customer is typically not buying a lone chip. It is buying a system, or a block of one, that can be linked with others into one giant computer.

Who is the customer?

You don't order one of these online. The buyers are companies, and a few of them matter a lot. Nvidia's own report says its revenue is concentrated among a limited number of customers.

“How limited?”

In the July quarter, one single customer paid 16 of every 100 dollars. Over the first half of its fiscal year, three customers paid 16, 15 and 13. That is 44 of every 100 dollars, from three buyers. In its last full fiscal year, the two largest paid 22 and 14 of every 100.

“Who are they?”

Nvidia doesn't name them. It only describes them. Some build the finished machines and sell them on. Others are cloud companies and AI model makers, buying for themselves. Behind them are the companies that end up using the machines. Nvidia calls them indirect customers. They include clouds, AI model makers, businesses and the public sector. The biggest group has a label: hyperscale. Nvidia uses it for the public clouds and the world's largest consumer internet companies.

In the July quarter it was 48.7 billion dollars, about half of everything Nvidia sold. A cloud company owns buildings full of computers and rents out their power over the internet. Nvidia's annual letter names four clouds it expects to be among the first to use its next system. They are AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure.

“And the AI apps I actually use?”

They can sit one step further back. Nvidia's report mentions one AI company, which it doesn't name. Nvidia estimates it added a meaningful amount of revenue by buying cloud services from Nvidia's customers. So the money moves along a chain. The AI company pays the cloud, the cloud pays for machines, and that money reaches Nvidia. One more clue: where the direct customers are based. $62 of our 100 come from companies headquartered in the United States. $28 come from Taiwan, and about 10 from everywhere else.

But Nvidia notes that the final user and the shipping address can be somewhere else. That answers question two: a short list of very large buyers.

Where do the $100 go?

Back to our 100 dollars. First, Nvidia pays for making the product. That takes 25.

“Its factories must be huge.”

It has no chip factories. Nvidia calls its approach fabless: it designs the chips, and other companies manufacture them. Fab is short for fabrication plant: a chip factory. The chips come from foundries such as Taiwan's TSMC. The memory comes from suppliers such as SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung. Contract manufacturers such as Hon Hai and Wistron then assemble, test and package the final products. So of every 100 dollars, 25 go out to make the product and 75 stay.

That share has a name, gross margin: 75 percent in the July quarter, against 72 a year earlier. Next come the people. At the end of fiscal 2026, Nvidia had about 42,000 employees, and 31,000 of them worked in research and development. Inventing the next products costs about 7 of our 100 dollars. Selling and running the company costs less than a dollar and a half. Then comes income tax: about 12. But one item goes the other way. Gains on Nvidia's investments in other companies added about 8.

Add it all up. 100 dollars of sales and about 8 of gains came in. 25 went to make the product, about 9 to run the company and about 12 to tax. That leaves 62 dollars of profit.

“62 out of 100? Is that normal?”

Compare Nvidia with itself. In fiscal 2024, its profit was about 49 for every 100 dollars of sales. In fiscal 2026, about 56. In the July quarter, 62. At each of those three points, it was higher than before. The filings can't tell you why customers pay these prices. They do show three features of its business: its design, its software and the whole system. One: it does the design, not the factory work. The factories belong to someone else.

Two: software. Nvidia introduced its programming system, CUDA, in 2006. It says more than 7.5 million developers now use its software tools. CUDA has been around for twenty years. Three: it sells the whole system, and it aims to bring out a new generation every year. Building all this takes time. Nvidia says its manufacturing lead times can stretch beyond twelve months for some products. So it orders far ahead. By late July it had committed 279 billion dollars to supply for the next several years.

For scale: making its products cost about 24 billion dollars in the July quarter. Those commitments are more than eleven times that. That answers question three. 25 to make it, about 9 to run the company and about 12 in tax. Add about 8 of investment gains, and the profit is 62.

“And what does Nvidia do with those 62?”

In that same quarter, Nvidia bought back its own shares. That is called a buyback. It cost about 20 of every 100 dollars of sales. It paid about 6 more to shareholders as dividends. It also invests in other companies and guarantees some partners' payments.

What could change it?

None of this is fixed. Nvidia's own filings point to three things that could change it: who buys, who competes, and what it has already ordered. First, the short list of buyers. When one customer is 16 of every 100 dollars, its plans matter. Second, some customers design hardware of their own. Nvidia lists Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft among its competitors. Third, the orders placed in advance. In fiscal 2026, Nvidia booked a 4.5 billion dollar charge for excess inventory and purchase obligations.

It was on one product line, as demand for it fell.

“So is the stock worth it?”

That is a question we don't answer here. No advice and no predictions. Just so you know how the money moves.
