Anthropic's IPO: who pays, who decides, who sells first

12 November 2026 · watch time 9 min · Watch on YouTube

Anthropic's IPO puts about $64 billion of shares up for sale at the top of its price range, through 22 banks led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley. On the side, seven tech companies, Nvidia and Google among them, have agreed to buy $26 billion more: no vote, no bank fee, no lock-up. Here's how the IPO book works, who's in charge afterwards and who can sell first.

Who pays, who's in charge, who sells first

Anthropic's IPO puts about 64 billion dollars of shares up for sale, at the top of its price range. Twenty-two banks are lined up to sell them. But another 26 billion dollars comes from somewhere else. Nvidia and Google have agreed to buy about 10 billion dollars each, on the side. No votes. No bank fee. No lock-up.

“My first case. Easy: the public pays, so the public's in charge.”

Not quite. One single share will carry more than half the votes on most matters. So who's really paying, who's in charge, and who gets to sell first?

How an IPO book works

An IPO is a company's first sale of shares to the public. After that, the shares trade on an exchange. Anthropic has applied for the Nasdaq, under the ticker ANT. Before it sells, the company publishes a price range. Anthropic's range is 70 to 78 dollars a share. There are 825 million shares on offer, almost all brand-new, so most of the money goes to Anthropic. At that range, the sale comes to 58 to 64 billion dollars, by our maths.

That would make it the second-biggest IPO on record, after SpaceX in June.

“But who decides the final price?”

Investors tell the banks how many shares they want, and at what price. Every order goes into one list: the book. The banks that keep it are the bookrunners. Here, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead all 22 banks. Each bank calls its own investors, and every order lands in the same book. A strong book can lift the price. A weak one can lower it. Then Anthropic, the selling co-founders and the lead banks agree the final price, just before trading starts.

The banks also get a greenshoe: they can sell 15 percent more shares than planned. That's 123.75 million extra. Morgan Stanley is in charge of using them to steady the price early on. If the price slips, the banks can buy shares back in the market, which helps hold it up. If it rises, they can get the extra shares from Anthropic instead. Up to 20 percent of the shares can go to ordinary savers. For that part, the filing names brokers such as Charles Schwab, Fidelity and Robinhood.

So far we have a price range, a book of orders, 22 banks, and an extra 15 percent. But that's not all. A second deal runs right next to this one.

Who writes the biggest checks

Seven tech companies have agreed to buy shares straight from Anthropic, in a private placement. Google and Nvidia put in about 10 billion dollars each. AMD adds about 2.5 billion, and Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft about a billion each. And TSMC, the chip maker, adds about 500 million. That's 26 billion dollars in total. They pay the same price as the IPO investors.

“Same price. So what's different?”

Their shares are Class N, and Class N shares carry no vote. If they're sold on, in the market for example, they become ordinary voting shares, called Class A. Anthropic pays the banks no fee on these shares. And they sign no lock-up, the promise most early owners make not to sell for months.

“And where does all the money go?”

Take 74 dollars a share, the middle of the range. Then Anthropic expects about 85 billion dollars from both deals, after costs. On where it goes, the official filing says general corporate purposes, including computing power. Anthropic's revenue was 17 billion dollars in the third quarter, about twelve times a year earlier, by our maths. In that quarter, it had an operating loss of 567 million dollars, after an operating profit the quarter before.

Its filing also lists about 900 billion dollars of computing commitments, stretching out to 2045.

“Nine hundred billion?”

Committed, not spent. About 71 billion of it falls due by September 2027, the filing says.

Who's in charge after the IPO

Here's the twist. After the IPO, Anthropic's ordinary shares come in five classes. Class A, the kind the public buys, get one vote each. There will be about 7.4 billion, mostly held by earlier owners. Class N shares, like the seven companies' new ones, get no vote. Class F is one single share, held by Founder LLC, an entity of the seven co-founders. That one share carries 50.1 percent of the votes on most matters. However many shares are issued, that one share keeps its 50.1 percent, for as long as Class F exists.

“One share with more than half the votes?”

Exactly. Then there's Class T, held by Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust. Through it, the trust elects a majority of the board. So public investors get a vote, but the board majority and more than half the votes are decided elsewhere.

“And what's the whole company worth, then?”

At 74 dollars, all the shares in issue after the IPO come to about 970 billion dollars, by our maths.

Who can sell first

After an IPO, early owners usually have to wait before they sell. That's the lock-up. Here, many early owners are locked up for up to 180 days. The co-founders and some big investors, for up to a year. A lock-up is meant to stop a flood of shares hitting the market all at once.

“So nobody sells early?”

Some can. About 3.6 billion locked-up shares could be freed early, the filing says. That includes up to 10 percent of what staff, past and present, and non-founder directors have locked up. They can sell that from the first day of trading.

“And the founders?”

Each of the seven co-founders sells about 3.4 million shares in the IPO itself. Their lock-up covers the shares they keep. That's 23.6 million shares in all, the only shares in the IPO that aren't new. At 74 dollars, that's about 250 million dollars each, by our maths. That money goes to them, not to Anthropic.

“So the public buys in, but control sits with one share and a trust.”

Exactly. And some early owners can sell from day one. Remember: these figures use the price range. The final price is set just before trading starts.
