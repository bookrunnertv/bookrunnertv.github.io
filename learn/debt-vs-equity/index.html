Finance From Zero · Lesson 1

Debt vs equity, explained with a lemonade stand

2 November 2026 · watch time 6 min · Watch on YouTube

In 2022, Elon Musk agreed to buy Twitter for $44 billion. He didn't pay it all out of his own pocket: banks lent part of the money, and the rest came from him and his partners. He used the two classic ways to raise money. Debt, and equity. Here is the difference, with a lemonade stand.

The takeaway

Debt is borrowed money you must pay back, with interest: the bill always comes, good year or bad. Equity is money from someone who gets a piece of the business: nothing to pay back, but you share the profits.

Sunny needs money

Meet Sunny. She sells lemonade, and people love it. Her stand makes a profit: what's left after paying for lemons and cups. And the line keeps getting longer.

A food truck would let her sell all over the park. It costs $10,000, and her savings are nowhere near that. To get the truck now, she needs someone else's money. She can borrow from a bank. Or find a partner.

Step 1Option one: borrow

The bank lends her the $10,000. Every year, she pays the bank $800 for using that money: that price is called interest. And after a few years, she gives the $10,000 back. That is debt: borrowed money you must pay back, with interest.

So the bank doesn't own any part of the truck? Not a bit, as long as she pays. If the truck makes a fortune, the bank still gets just its $800.

But imagine it rains all summer, and nobody buys lemonade. The bank still wants its $800. The truck is the bank's safety net: if she can't pay, the bank takes it and sells it. With debt, the bill always comes. Fine in a good year. Dangerous in a bad one.

Step 2Option two: a partner

Her neighbor, Max, gives her the $10,000. He doesn't want interest. He doesn't want the money back. He wants a piece of the business: 40 percent of it. That is equity: money from someone who gets a piece of the business. In finance, equity means ownership.

So Max's money is free? It looks free. If the truck fails, Max can lose all his money, and Sunny owes him nothing. But if it's a hit, 40 percent of the profit goes to Max, every year, for as long as he owns his piece. And Max gets a say in how the business is run.

Equity: no fixed payments, nothing to pay back. But you share the profits.

Step 3Which one costs less?

It depends on the profit. Say the business makes $5,000 of profit a year. The bank costs $800. Max's 40 percent costs $2,000. Paying back the $10,000 isn't a cost: it was the bank's money all along. So in a good year, debt is cheaper.

What it costs Sunny each yearGood year ($5,000 profit)Bad year (no profit)
The bank loan (debt)$800$800
Max's 40% (equity)$2,000$0

Now a bad year: no profit at all. The bank still wants its $800. Max gets nothing. Very sure the lemonade will sell? Borrow: it costs less. Not sure? Take a partner: a bad year won't sink her.

Many businesses use both: a loan and a partner. If it fails, everything is sold, and the bank is paid first. The owners split whatever is left. Often, nothing.

Key terms

Profit
What's left after paying the costs. For Sunny: after paying for lemons and cups.
Interest
The price you pay to use borrowed money.
Debt
Borrowed money you must pay back, with interest.
Equity
Money from owners, for a piece of the business. No duty to pay it back.

The real thing: Twitter

Back to Musk. His deal used both. About $13 billion was debt: bank loans that Twitter itself would owe. Most of the money was equity, from Musk and his partners.

Remember the debt rule? The bill always comes. After the deal, Twitter had to pay about $1.2 billion a year in interest. Good year or bad, it was due. Just like Sunny's $800.

What you know now

  1. Debt is borrowed money: you pay it back with interest, good year or bad.
  2. Equity is owners' money: you never have to pay it back, but you share the profits.
  3. And if the business fails, lenders like the bank are paid before owners.
