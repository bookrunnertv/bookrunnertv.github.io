What is a bookrunner?

In short

A bookrunner is the bank that runs the order book and prices the deal. When a company sells shares or bonds to investors, the bookrunner collects the orders (how many, and at what price), sets one price for all, and decides who gets what.

Why it's called running the book

The book is the list of investors' orders. Think of a concert presale: fans say how many tickets they want, and the most they would pay. In an IPO, the banks do that with big investors. Then they set one price for all, and pick who gets shares. That is bookbuilding. More orders than shares on offer, and the book is oversubscribed.

Why not just sell to whoever pays the most? Because the seller also picks its owners. Price is only half the decision. The other half is who gets the shares.

What a bookrunner does, step by step

Here is the job in a real deal: Figma's IPO, in July 2025, with Morgan Stanley leading the banks as bookrunner.

  1. Pitch the deal. On 21 July 2025 the roadshow began: the banks pitched Figma to big funds at $25 to $28 a share.
  2. Collect the orders. Each fund was asked how many shares it wanted and the most it would pay; for Figma, the banks reportedly insisted on both. Every order goes into the book.
  3. Read the demand. The orders poured in. On 28 July the banks raised the range to $30 to $32. Demand was close to forty times the shares on offer, Bloomberg reported.
  4. Set one price. On the evening of 30 July the price was set at $33, a dollar above the raised range: about 37 million shares, for $1.2 billion.
  5. Choose the owners. Reportedly, about half the investors who ordered got nothing, and about 25 investors took 70% of the shares, IFR reported.
  6. Sell extra shares if the deal allows. Demand was so strong that the banks sold 5.5 million extra shares from the sellers, as the deal allowed (the greenshoe, 15% of the base deal), at the same $33.

The next day, Figma's first on the stock market, the shares closed at $115.50: up 250%. Whether $33 was too cheap is the question our Figma IPO episode takes apart.

Lead, joint and co-managers: reading the names on a deal

Big deals can have several bookrunners, and the order of the names matters. The left-most name on a deal is the lead bookrunner: it runs the process.

Figma's final prospectus lists nine firms on its cover. Four were joint lead book-running managers: Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, Allen & Company and J.P. Morgan, with Morgan Stanley first. William Blair and Wolfe | Nomura Alliance were co-managers.

On the loans behind Twitter's buyout, in 2022, Morgan Stanley was the left lead arranger; Bank of America, Barclays, MUFG, BNP Paribas, Mizuho and Société Générale were lead arrangers and joint bookrunners. Morgan Stanley also carried the biggest slice: about 27% of the $13 billion, roughly $3.5 billion.

A bank can even run its own book. In June 2022, Credit Suisse sold a new AT1 bond of its own, as sole lead, with a 9.75% coupon, and raised $1.65 billion. Nine months later, that bond was worth nothing.

Bookrunner or underwriter?

Running the book is about orders and price. Underwriting is a promise: to fund the deal even if no investor shows up. On Twitter's buyout, the seven banks put that promise in writing: they were committed to provide $13 billion. They did not promise to try to find it; they promised to fund it. If the market closes tomorrow, you still wire the money.

That promise is where the risk sits. About $6 billion of Twitter's debt was meant to come from bonds sold to big investors. The bonds never sold, so the seven banks lent that money themselves, and kept Twitter's debt on their books for thirty months. Bankers call that a hung deal.

On Silicon Valley Bank's share sale, in March 2023, the prospectus named Goldman Sachs and SVB Securities, SVB's own investment-banking arm, as joint book-running managers, with Goldman as the representative of the underwriters. Because SVB Securities was SVB's own subsidiary, the prospectus flagged a conflict of interest under FINRA Rule 5121.

How bookrunners get paid

  • Figma: the underwriting discount was $1.485 a share, 4.5% of the $33 price. On every share sold, the extra shares included, that is about $63 million for the banks. The money left on the table on day one was about 56 times as much.
  • Twitter: the lenders' arrangement fees were never disclosed. Bloomberg reported that such fees typically range from 2% to 3.5% of the financing: a few hundred million dollars on $13 billion, against thirty months of risk.
  • Silicon Valley Bank: by Goldman's account, the underwriting fee would only have been earned if the deal closed. The share sale was called off before it was priced, and Goldman says it received no underwriting fee.

The fee is never the whole story: a bank that promises the money can end up holding the risk.

Quick answers

Is a bookrunner the same as an underwriter?

Not exactly. Running the book means collecting the orders, setting the price and choosing who gets what. Underwriting is the promise to fund the deal even if no investor shows up. The same banks can do both: on Twitter's buyout, the seven banks that arranged the loans also committed the money.

What is the lead bookrunner?

The bank that runs the process. On a deal, its name comes first, on the left. On the loans behind Twitter's buyout, that was Morgan Stanley, which also carried the biggest slice.

What is a joint bookrunner?

One of several banks that run the book together. Figma's IPO had four joint lead book-running managers: Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, Allen & Company and J.P. Morgan.

What is bookbuilding?

Collecting orders first, then setting one price for all. Each investor says how many shares it wants and the most it would pay; the bookrunner sets the price and picks who gets shares.

Can a bank be its own bookrunner?

Yes. In June 2022, Credit Suisse ran its own book, as sole lead, for its own 9.75% AT1 bond.
