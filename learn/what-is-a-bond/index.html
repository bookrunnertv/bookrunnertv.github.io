What is a bond? Loans and bonds, explained with a lemonade stand

9 November 2026 · watch time 5 min · Watch on YouTube

Twitter, 2022. To help pay for Musk's deal, the plan was to sell bonds to investors: about $6 billion of them. They never sold. So what is a bond? And how did seven banks get stuck holding them? Here it is, with a lemonade stand.

The takeaway

A bond is one small piece of a big loan: whoever holds it is a lender, paid interest every year and the full amount back on the end date. A share makes you an owner; a bond makes you a lender.

Step 1Every loan has a price

Sunny wants a second truck. In Lesson 1, she took a partner. This time, she asks her bank for a loan.

A loan usually has three parts. How much: $10,000. The price, called interest: $800 a year. And an end date: in five years, she pays the $10,000 back.

And if she can't pay? Then the bank can take the truck, and sell it to get paid. That truck has a name: collateral. Something the lender can take if you don't pay.

Step 2Too big for one bank

Then Sunny dreams bigger: a small lemonade factory. She needs $100,000. Her bank says no: that's more than it lends to any one customer. So she splits it into 100 small loans, of $1,000 each. Now lots of people can lend her a little. Each one risks only $1,000.

Each piece is called a bond: one small piece of a big loan. Unlike a share from Lesson 2, it's not a piece of the business. If something goes wrong, the loss is shared by many lenders. Not one bank losing a lot.

Can you buy one? Sure. Buying a bond means lending. You lend Sunny $1,000. Each year, she pays you $80 of interest. And on the end date, you get your $1,000 back. The rule: with bonds, one big loan can come from many lenders at once.

Step 3Bond or share?

Remember Max? He owns shares in Sunny's Lemonade. You own a bond. Max is an owner: in a great year, his slice of the profit can be big. You're a lender: great year or not, you get your $80. No more. But if the business fails, lenders get paid first. Owners get whatever is left.

Like shares, bonds can be sold before the end date. The buyer then gets the $80 a year, and the $1,000 at the end. If people fear Sunny won't pay, buyers pay less than $1,000.

So which is better? Neither. A bond is steadier. A share can win more, or lose more. The rule: a share makes you an owner. A bond makes you a lender.

Key terms

Loan
Borrowed money that usually has three parts: how much, a price (the interest) and an end date. Often, collateral too.
Interest
The price you pay to use borrowed money.
Collateral
Something the lender can take and sell if you don't pay.
Bond
One small piece of a big loan. Whoever holds it is a lender.

The real thing: Twitter

Back to Twitter. In 2022, seven banks agreed to lend about $13 billion for Musk's deal. About $6 billion was meant to come from bonds Twitter would sell to big investors. Like Sunny's.

The seven banks promised: if the bonds don't sell, we lend that money ourselves. But by then, investors could get more interest elsewhere, and Twitter looked risky. Nobody wanted those bonds at full price.

So the banks lent that money themselves. Selling those loans cheap meant a big loss, so they held on, for over two years. If Twitter couldn't pay, the lenders' loss would fall on just seven banks. Not on many lenders, a little each.

What you know now

  1. A loan usually has an amount, a price, and an end date. Often, collateral too.
  2. A bond is one small piece of a big loan.
  3. A share makes you an owner. A bond makes you a lender.

Quick quiz

You buy one of Sunny's bonds from someone, for $900. If Sunny pays everything, what do you get back on the end date?

Show the answer

$1,000, the bond's full amount. You paid $900, so that's $100 more. Plus $80 a year until then.
