What does an investment bank actually do? Explained with a lemonade stand

11 November 2026 · watch time 6 min · Watch on YouTube

In 2022, a bank helped Elon Musk buy Twitter. In 2025, the same bank helped sell Figma's shares to the public. That bank is Morgan Stanley: same bank, two very different jobs. So what does an investment bank actually do? And what is a bookrunner? Here it is, with a lemonade stand.

Premieres on YouTube on 11 November 2026, 14:15 New York time. Subscribe to get it the moment it is out.

The takeaway

An investment bank helps companies raise money, and buy or sell companies. The bookrunner is the bank that keeps the book of investors' orders and, with the company's OK, decides who gets what.

Step 1A bank for big moves

Sunny's Lemonade keeps growing: more trucks, a small factory, lots of customers. Now she wants $1 million, to open stands in new towns. Her plan: add new shares to the old ones, and sell them to investors. A share, from Lesson 2: one equal piece of a company.

Her local bank lends money. But finding buyers for shares isn't its job. For that, she needs another kind of bank: an investment bank. A bank that helps companies raise money, and buy or sell companies.

Inside, lots of teams. Here are three of its big jobs.

  1. Advisers help companies buy or sell other companies.
  2. Teams that sell new shares and bonds, to raise money for companies. A bond, from Lesson 3: one small piece of a big loan.
  3. The trading floor, where the bank trades shares and bonds with investors.

Isn't a bank where you keep your savings? That's another kind of bank. This one works mostly for companies and big investors. So it's a team of helpers, for big money moves? Roughly. And it gets paid for each job.

The rule: an investment bank helps companies raise money, and buy or sell companies.

Step 2The book

Sunny hires the investment bank to sell her new shares. First, its sales team calls big investors, and tells them Sunny's story. This sale isn't on the stock market yet: only investors the bank knows.

Investors who like it place an order: how much they want to buy. Every order goes into one list, called the book. For days, the calls go on, and the list grows. The bank that keeps the book is called the bookrunner. That's where this channel gets its name.

Sunny's book fills up: orders for $3 million. She's only selling $1 million. So who gets the shares? With Sunny's OK, the bookrunner decides who gets how many. Some investors get less than they asked for. Some get none.

Like concert tickets, with more fans than seats? Roughly. But there's no queue: the bookrunner chooses. Then the swap: the shares go to the investors, and the money to Sunny.

Bonds work the same way. Lenders place orders, and the bookrunner keeps the book. The rule: the bookrunner keeps the book. With the company's OK, it decides who gets what.

Step 3Advisers and traders

Back to the advisers. Say Sunny wants to buy the juice stand next door. They help her agree on a price, and close the deal.

Job three: the trading floor, a big room of screens and phones. Once sold, shares and bonds keep changing hands between investors. Investors phone the bank's sales people with their orders. The bank's traders buy from investors, or sell to them, right away.

And its research team writes reports on companies, like Sunny's.

And who pays the bank for all this? Its clients pay fees. Sunny pays a small cut of the money raised. The rule: one bank, many jobs. Advise, raise money, and trade.

Key terms

Investment bank
A bank that helps companies raise money, and buy or sell companies.
Book (order book)
The one list where every investor's order goes: how much each one wants to buy.
Bookrunner
The bank that keeps the book and, with the company, decides who gets what.
Trading floor
A big room of screens and phones, where the bank trades shares and bonds with investors.

The real thing: Morgan Stanley

Back to Morgan Stanley. In 2022, it was Elon Musk's main adviser on buying Twitter. Investment banks lend too: Morgan Stanley headed the group of banks lending about $13 billion for the deal. The loans from Lesson 3.

In 2025, Figma's shares went on the stock market for the first time. Figma hired several bookrunners to keep its book, and Morgan Stanley was the main one. Reports said investors asked for about 40 times the shares on sale. Sunny's book was three times what she sold.

So, as with Sunny, the bookrunners had to choose who got shares.

What you know now

  1. An investment bank helps companies raise money, and buy or sell companies.
  2. The bookrunner keeps the book. With the company's OK, it decides who gets what.
  3. On the trading floor, the bank trades shares and bonds with investors.
